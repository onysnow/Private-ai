What changed, and why

  • No empty canvas. The drawing is as tall as its tallest column, not a fixed 600 px box, and the side key became the strip above, so the columns take the full width.
  • Column order now follows the links. Before, people–companies–organisations–property put Priya's link to Harborline across the Companies column and through Meridian's name. Ordering columns so linked kinds are neighbours (organisations · people · companies · property) means every link here joins neighbouring columns: no line crosses a name. The same principle as hive plots' axis ordering (Krzywinski et al. 2012) and as crossing reduction in layered drawing (Sugiyama et al. 1981).
  • Links leave a dot sideways. Names sit under the dots, so a link that leaves and arrives horizontally never runs through a name. Links between two parties of the same kind (Bellcastle owns and paid Meridian) curve out to the column's side, labelled, instead of running vertically over the names.
  • Each column says what is in it: a count, and where its parties are registered, as small chips. The band between two columns counts the links that cross it, by kind, so “Companies → Property: 1 owns” reads before any line is traced.
  • The red “!” sits on the payment's own curve, clear of names. Direction is the tapered width (wide end at the source) agreed in pass 2; no arrowheads.
  • Pointing at a party gives its card with Research in connection with…, Research this party, Tag and Note; pointing at a column lights its parties and links; pointing at a band lights the links it counts.

What changed, and why

  • One panel for every source. “OpenAleph corpus” and “External connectors” were two panels for the same work: finding out whether a party appears somewhere else. Now OpenAleph, OpenSanctions, the outside Aleph server, Firecrawl, your documents and the planned free registries sit in one place, with one queue of things to decide. The split by tool was how the code was organised, not how a reporter works.
  • Where a record came from is a tag on it, not the panel it sits in. The tags come from what the app already stores: every fact on a party is a statement with its dataset (a document, a source, or you), so a party's tags are the sources of its facts. Nothing new to keep in step.
  • Tags are filters everywhere. Click one here, or pick sources in the filter row, and the queue, the counts and the lists narrow to them. The same source filter sits in search, the network charts, the timeline, the chart builder and exports, so “show me only what OpenSanctions and the registry filing say” is one click on any page.
  • Each source states its condition first (connected, off, local copy, planned, paid) so nothing that cannot run looks usable. Nielsen's first heuristic, visibility of system status (1994).
  • The pipeline counts are split by source, so it shows where work is piling up and which source it is from.

What changed, and why

  • Who he is and what can be done, first. The name, kind, nationality and tags in a header, with the actions in one row: research, tag, note, edit. Before, “Edit entity” was a black block on a beige card and the counts ran together in one grey line.
  • Six tiles say how much there is and how sure, in the status colours: before you read anything, you see one link, one claim not yet shown, one piece of evidence nobody has reviewed.
  • Connections as a picture, with a second ring (who Meridian is linked to), drawn in the network's codes. The empty-chart bug is fixed for people.
  • Every property with its source, and a gap (date of birth) with the lookup that would fill it.
  • Empty sections become one line, not a heading over nothing: merges, identity decisions and conflicts share one strip. Leads, tasks and timeline headings with nothing under them are gone.
  • The right column is about what to do next: red flags, lookups (with why these sources), research runs, notes and leads.
